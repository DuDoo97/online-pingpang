What was built

A browser demo in Online Pingpang/: Three.js for the picture, a custom 1,300-line physics and rules core underneath. You control the racket with the mouse, toss with a click, and play a first-to-11 game against an AI that predicts the ball using the very same physics. Every physical parameter is a live slider in the side panel, so you can watch what happens when Magnus is doubled or air is removed.

Run it

npm install
npm run dev      # http://localhost:5173
npm test         # 20 physics checks vs. published numbers
npm run single   # dist/pingpang.html, one double-clickable file

Controls

  • Mouse moves the racket on a 45° plane: up on the screen is up and forward.
  • Swing through the ball. Racket speed sets pace, its brushing motion sets spin.
  • click / space toss the serve. Q / E or wheel close / open the face.
  • Shot assist slider: 0 % is raw physics, 60 % default corrects a swing toward the nearest legal shot within a budget.

Seven things the physics literature insists on

  1. Air is the dominant force, not gravity. At 20 m/s drag is about five times the ball's weight. Speed halves every ~5 m of flight and terminal velocity is only ~9 m/s. A trajectory computed in vacuum is roughly three times too long. (Conti et al. 2026; Spin Doctor sim)
  2. Magnus lift is gravity-scale. A loop at 15 m/s with 600 rad/s of topspin feels ~2.5 g of downward force. That is why hard topspin still lands and why backspin balls float. The lift coefficient grows with spin parameter S = rω/v but saturates around 0.4–0.5 (Miyazaki's "lift crisis"), so it must be capped or 150 rps balls turn into cartoons.
  3. Spin barely decays in flight (3–10 %/s), speed loses 30–50 %. So S rises along the flight and loops dive hardest near the end. Constant-spin flight is an acceptable approximation.
  4. Restitution falls with impact speed. Table: e ≈ 0.98 − 0.02·|v|, so 0.88 at 5 m/s and 0.78 at 10 m/s (the shell buckles). Racket: e ≈ 0.878 − 0.020·|v|. Constant COR makes smashes bounce like pushes.
  5. The bounce couples spin and speed. With μ = 0.25 a heavy topspin ball grips, converts ~20 % of its spin into ~7 % more forward speed and comes out lower (Kamijima 2013 bench data). Backspin does the reverse and, slow enough, bounces back toward the net. A no-spin ball leaves every bounce with topspin.
  6. Rubber is a high-grip spring, not a friction surface. Tangential restitution et ≈ 0.8 means the contact point rebounds ("over-grip"). Outgoing spin is set almost entirely by the racket's tangential velocity: brushing at 10 m/s on a dead ball yields ~525 rad/s, about 5,000 rpm.
  7. Contacts last 0.7 ms (table) and ~1.5 ms (racket). Far below any frame. Treat them as instantaneous impulses; do not integrate through them.

Two patterns are universal. Every serious project writes its own ball physics instead of using cannon-es, Rapier or Ammo: with a 2.7 g ball at 30 m/s and a 1.4 cm racket those engines tunnel, and none model Magnus or spin-coupled bounce. And every non-VR game layers a shot solver under the player's intent, because a mouse cannot supply a real 3D swing.

Design decisions in this demo

  • Custom analytical physics, 360 Hz, swept collisions. Four bodies do not need an engine. The racket test sweeps both the ball and the racket over the step, so a 30 m/s ball cannot pass through the blade.
  • Shot assist is a budget, not a blend. Averaging a raw velocity with a legal one usually produces an illegal shot, because the set of legal launches is not convex. Instead a solver finds the legal shot closest to your swing, keeps your spin, and applies it fully if the correction fits a budget that grows with the slider. Beyond the budget the ball is only pulled part-way and will probably miss. At 0 % you get raw physics.
  • The AI plays the same physics. After a reaction delay (0.10–0.28 s by difficulty) it forward-simulates the incoming ball to its strike plane, adds aim noise, then searches launch elevation for a target on your side. Difficulty is reaction, noise and a miss chance, never a faster ball.
  • Amateur by design. Spin in play tops out around 4,000–5,000 rpm and speed around 20 m/s, the club range. Pro numbers (100–150 rps, 25–35 m/s) are reachable only by moving the physics sliders, and no serve-motion or footwork systems were added.
  • Spin is made legible. An orange seam rotates with the ball, the trail shows curvature, and a badge reports speed, rpm and spin type after every hit.
  • Rules kept honest. Serve must bounce on your side first, a net-clipping serve is a let, volleys lose the point, service alternates every two points and every point from 10–10.
Verified end to end: a headless scripted player against the medium AI produces rallies up to 70 shots with both sides winning points, and a Playwright-driven browser session tosses, hits and clears the net.

What is still rough

  • Mouse control cannot express a horizontal brush on the ball's side, so sidespin comes mostly from lateral swing direction and the racket's yaw.
  • The AI's racket motion is cosmetic: its contact resolves to the shot it solved. The racket still travels to the intercept, so misses look like misses.
  • The demo is single-player. The research notes in docs/ lay out the netplay approach (host-authoritative, 30 Hz snapshots, delayed authoritative ball spawn) if online play is the next step.
